Simulated markets are built from real NVDA 5-minute trading days from the last 60 days, stitched together in a random order (same intraday swings, overnight gaps and volume), with a trend added for bull or bear. The live bot's own code trades them with a simulated clock. "New random market" builds a different one.
Real past data replays real past bars through the exact same code the live bot runs. Each bar the bot checks its stop, then its exit rule, then (if flat) its entry rule. Green triangles are buys, red are sells, the dotted red line is the stop.

Would it have worked? Backtest of the exact same rules

Honest caveat: these tests use today's list of stocks, which leaves out companies that went bankrupt or shrank. That makes every result look better than reality. The equal-weight row holds every stock on the same list, so it has the same bias: the fair test is whether the scanner beats that row, not just the S&P 500.

How it picks

  1. Every weekday morning it pulls the full list of ~7,000 US stocks from Nasdaq and keeps real companies (worth $2B+, priced $5+, about 2,100 stocks).
  2. It scores each one by how fast and how steadily it has climbed over the last 90 trading days (Clenow's trend slope x smoothness; steady climbers keep going more often than one-day spikes).
  3. It skips stocks below their 100-day average, more than 25% under their 52-week high, that jumped 15%+ in a day recently, or that trade too little.
  4. It only buys while the S&P 500 is above its 200-day average. In a falling market it buys nothing new.
  5. It holds the top 10 in equal amounts. It sells a stock the morning after it closes below its 100-day average (the trend broke), and at each rebalance (every 4 weeks) it swaps out anything that fell out of the top 20.
  6. It decides after the close and trades at the next open, paying 0.05% per trade, like a real account.

What the bot knows: advice from traders and research, and what survived testing

Every rule was tested on hundreds of simulated NVDA markets and on real data from 6-12 stocks (including ones that crashed). Rules marked "dropped" sounded good but made the bot worse.

Two strategies, side by side

Plain is the assignment: buy when the 20-bar average crosses above the 50-bar average (golden cross), sell when it crosses back below (death cross). All in, no stop.

Swing is what the live bot runs now. It buys when NVDA closes above its highest price of the last 2 trading days (156 five-minute bars) and sells on a close below the last 1-day low, or if a wide trailing stop (12x ATR, about 3-4% below the peak) is hit. It was picked because it held up in hundreds of simulated markets; the faster settings traded too often and lost to costs.

Smart crossover keeps the assignment's crossover and adds the risk rules from the playbook:

  • Trend filter: only buy when price is above the 200-bar average, so it sits out bear markets.
  • RSI guard: skip entries when RSI(14) is above 75 (already overheated).
  • ATR stop: initial stop 2.5x the average true range below entry.
  • Trailing stop: stop ratchets up to 5x ATR below the highest price since entry. It never moves down.
  • Volatility sizing: position is sized so hitting the stop loses at most 10% of the account, capped at 100% (no leverage). In calm markets that means full size, when the stock goes wild it buys less.
  • Costs: 0.05% per trade for commission and slippage.

The honest part: on stocks that went up 10x or more (NVDA, AMD), nothing here beats just buying and holding. Timing rules spend time out of the market and miss big up days. What the smart rules buy you is smaller drawdowns. Check the Backtest tab and compare Max drawdown and Sharpe, not only total return. Also: backtesting only on a stock we already know was a huge winner flatters any long-only strategy.

How to make it better (what the tests showed)

I tested four strategies on NVDA, AMD, SPY, AAPL, MSFT and TSLA on 5-minute, 1-hour and daily bars, splitting each history in half so a rule had to work in both halves.

  1. Timeframe matters more than the indicator. On 5-minute bars every strategy lost money after costs on most stocks. On daily bars every strategy made money in both halves on most stocks. Short bars are mostly noise.
  2. Costs eat fast bots alive. The smart bot made about 70 trades in 60 days on 5-minute bars. At 0.05% per side that is roughly 7% of the account, which turned small wins into losses on 5 of 6 stocks.
  3. Pullback buying is the best risk-adjusted rule on daily bars. Buying short dips (RSI(2) under 10) while above the 200-day average and selling the bounce gave NVDA about 13% a year with a -19% worst drop, while sitting in cash 88% of the time.
  4. Breakouts beat crossovers on most stocks on daily bars, with smaller drawdowns, because they react faster to new highs.
  5. Nothing beat buy-and-hold on big winners. The rules earn their keep in crashes (TSLA second half: holding fell 72% at worst, pullback 32%).

Next steps that would help most: trade daily signals but check stops every 5 minutes, test on stocks that went down too (not only winners), and combine pullback plus breakout so the bot has something to do in both quiet and trending markets.
